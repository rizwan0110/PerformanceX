## Live in a valued direction

Living one's life in harmony with what is valuable and meaningful to one, can be an important source of well-being. In this context, the word "values" comes up. Values are fundamental attitudes to things that are important to a person, what they think is important and good. Metaphorically, values are often described as a beacon or a compass that helps us with the direction of life. Questions such as "How do I want to be perceived by others?" or "How do I want to be remembered?" are questions that can help you identify your values. Values are thus more about the "how", i.e. how we want to behave, and not about what we achieve or can do.

The latter, the "what", is rather related to goals. Most elite athletes are used to working with goals and it usually has a performance-enhancing and motivating function. Goals are set, we work to achieve them, we achieve some and we set new goals. However, goals are always in the future, and sometimes goal setting can create a feeling of chaos, which can then be perceived as stressful. As a complement to goal setting, we therefore recommend also working with values in life and specifically in sports. Values and goals can be combined with each other!

Connecting with your values can be especially helpful in difficult moments. When things are tough, you can always connect to what is really important, and take small steps in the form of concrete actions to reconnect with what you value. At the same time, living in a valued direction is good for actively creating a meaningful life and meaningful sports. Living in the direction of one's values is perceived as satisfying and increases the likelihood of positive reinforcement in everyday life, which boosts motivation and well-being.

## What can you do to identify and work with your values.

What can you do yourself?
Try to clarify your values. You can do this using one of the suggested exercises below, or by reflecting on the following questions: "How do I want my coach and teammates/training buddies/opponents to perceive me?" and "How do I want to be remembered?". Try to describe your values based on the exercise you have done, in the form of a few words, a short sentence or a "motto".
Once you have identified your values, take a step further and concretize your values: Take one value at a time and think about what concrete actions you can take to move in the direction of your value. List all the documents that you can think of in connection with each value. A few examples: If you have a value to develop all the time, a concrete action can be to evaluate after the next training session how it went, what went well, what went less well, and reflect on what you have learned new today. If your value is to live a sustainable life, a concrete action can be to carry out a relaxation exercise every day after lunch. If your value is professionalism, a concrete action can be to go to bed at 10 p.m. every night to get enough sleep. To come up with concrete actions, ask yourself the following question: "What can I do in the next 24 hours to take a step towards my values?" Be as concrete as possible by specifying actions (not how you should feel or think).You can remind yourself of your values by writing them down in a place you see often, such as on a poster in the gym, on your water bottle, or by drawing a picture that represents your values. Use your imagination to find a solution that works for you. Feel free to evaluate whether you are living in the direction of your values at regular intervals.
