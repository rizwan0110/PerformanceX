## Social relations

One area of importance for our mental health is our social relationships. Positively perceived social relationships can make us feel good. However, not everyone has the same need for relationships or social interactions, but it is individual.

What you can do to optimize your mental health, among other things, is to first and foremost take care of your good relationships with people close to you, such as family members, friends, teammates or clubmates or work colleagues. However, it is easier said than done, sometimes it can be perceived as difficult to spend time and energy on social relationships. You may feel that you do not have time, or prioritize other things that in the long run may be less rewarding. Or you meet people "physically", but are still not really present in the conversations; You may be worried about the next challenge or busy with your mobile, which usually leads to the meeting not being perceived as very rewarding.

Because of the important role that social relationships play in our mental health, it can be helpful to think about how you can develop and maintain the way you interact with the people around you who are important in your life.

What can you do to improve Social relations?
- Think about which people in your social network you would like to meet more. Contact them and schedule a meeting. If you feel that time is short, maybe you can meet over lunch.
- Think about how you might meet new people. Maybe you can sign up for a course where you potentially meet new people? Or you try to be open in connection with gatherings, meetings and invitations to see if there is anyone who might feel interesting to get to know better. Sometimes it's enough to pay attention to the people you already know well in your environment. Maybe it can be interesting to spend a little more time with the new player in the team?
- Try to be fully present in the moment when you do meet other people. The quality of the social meetings that take place, and the perceived outcome, depends a lot on whether you are present in the moment. If you're not distracted by your phone (a tip is to put it away so it's not visible or set it to do not disturb) or your own thoughts, and can fully concentrate on what's being said and done during the call, you'll probably get more out of the meeting. That way, you'll also feel more connected to the person you're talking to.
- Develop your conversational skills. Ask your conversation partner how they are doing in life, listen carefully and do not jump in with advice and tips on how they can deal with their challenges. Learn active listening, where you listen, summarize and then repeat what you have heard. This leads to your conversation partner realizing that you have listened fully and understood what they have said. Make sure the person feels that they have been heard, accepted, and understood. Then they might do the same with you.
- Dare to talk about yourself too. Tell them how you feel and what you deal with in life. Don't be afraid to talk about challenging things or situations in your life, it can create an open conversation climate and open up for the exchange of thoughts about life's challenges. Maybe the person you're talking to has experienced something similar to yours, but hasn't yet dared to talk about it?
- Keep in mind that all people can make mistakes sometimes. Don't be afraid to apologize if you've done something less good to someone else. And likewise, be open to other people's apologies if they have messed up something. It is an important skill, among other things, to be able to maintain social relationships even during stormy times.
