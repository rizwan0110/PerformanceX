In sports, there has traditionally been a great focus on the male athlete, and for many years most research and development in sports has been done on men. But fortunately, this has started to change in recent years. Topics that mainly concern female athletes, such as the menstrual cycle and pregnancy, have begun to be discussed. On this page, we will highlight specific issues related to the well-being and performance of female athletes.

## Premenstrual syndrome/premenstrual dysphoric syndrome
The fact that the female body and well-being are affected by the hormone cycle has been known since ancient times. How much women are affected by the hormonal fluctuations is individual. 80-95% of all menstruating women feel their hormone cycle in some form, while 30-40% suffer from PMS, premenstrual syndrome.

# Premenstrual syndrome Symptom : Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) is the name given to the physical and mental problems that last a number of days before your period starts. Some people experience problems a few days before their period, while others are also affected around ovulation, which occurs about two weeks before their period. PMS symptoms usually stop during the first three days of menstruation. PMS can manifest itself in many different ways and the symptoms can also vary from cycle to cycle. In PMS, you may have one or more of the symptoms below.

## Psychological symptoms are: 
- You are irritable and irritable.
- You feel depressed, tearful and have a reduced desire to do things.
- You have mood swings.
- You may find it more difficult to sleep.
- You easily get caught up in negative thoughts.
- You feel anxiety and worry.
- You may have more difficulty concentrating, which can affect you in your sports practice.

## Bodily symptoms are: 

- You feel tired and have less energy, which can cause difficulties in training and competition.
- Your body becomes swollen.
- You have swollen and sore breasts.
- You get a headache.
- You may get hungrier than usual and be drawn to fast carbs.
- You may feel clumsy and have impaired balance, which can affect coordination in your sport.
- Premenstrual dysphoric syndrome (PMDD) is a more severe form of PMS that affects 3–5% of menstruating women. To be diagnosed with PMDD, five of the mental symptoms above must apply to you, one of the top four. The symptoms should also be so severe that everyday life is negatively affected, and subside in connection with the onset of menstruation.

## What causes Premenstrual syndrome Symptom?
It is not known for sure why some people suffer from PMS. The research so far indicates that it seems that some women are more sensitive to the waste products that are formed when hormones are broken down in the body during the menstrual cycle. These residues indirectly affect women's moods and bodies to different extents.

## What can you do in case of Premenstrual syndrome Symptom ? 
There are several areas that you can work on yourself. To be able to do this, you need to get to know your symptoms according to the first point below.

- Chart at least two menstrual cycles. By mapping your mental and physical symptoms throughout the month, you can detect if there are patterns in your symptoms. Feel free to note your experience of training during each day of the menstrual cycle to see if there is a connection between where you are in the cycle and your performance. To help, you can use special apps such as Clue, Flo, or an app called FitrWoman where you can also map your training.
- Reduce stress and high demands. PMS seems to be more common in people who have high demands on themselves and on performing perfectly. Practice lowering the expectations you have of yourself and of others.
- Avoid the compensation trap. What we can cope with and cope with on normal days can be very different from the days we have PMS. A common strategy is to overexert yourself on the days you feel good to compensate for the days when you don't have as much energy. This can lead to you going into the upcoming PMS period more tired than usual. The tiredness itself makes it even more difficult for us to restrain our temper and we more easily end up in negative thoughts. This pattern leads to the fluctuations with peaks and troughs in both energy level and mood. By slowing down a little on the days you feel good and adapting the training level to your daily fitness on the days you have PMS, you can get a better balance both physically and mentally. It's about accepting that you don't have as much energy throughout the month and that you sometimes need to lower your demands to maintain the feeling of being in control of the situation.
- Practice mindfulness regularly. Mindfulness is about the ability to be here and now, in the moment. It is a well-proven way to reduce stress and create distance from negative thoughts. Mindfulness helps you manage your thoughts in a better way and is a powerful tool on the way to starting to accept them, your feelings, and yourself as you are.
- Review your triggers. Maybe you've noticed that certain situations trigger you particularly much when you have PMS. One method that can be helpful is to consciously avoid certain triggers during the worst days. If this is not possible, you can reduce the strength of them by adapting the situation around them. In general, avoidance tends to worsen anxiety and depression in the long run because it tends to lead to more and more avoidance. In PMS, on the other hand, temporary avoidance can be a very effective strategy. For example, it can be about avoiding social media on certain days, avoiding making important decisions or discussions, or avoiding certain exercise elements.
- Problem solving. To deal with unavoidable triggers, problem-solving can be a good method. Once you know what your problem is, you can think of alternative solutions. Try not to censor yourself, but pour out as many suggestions as possible. Then select the suggestions that are most reasonable and test them one or more cycles. Can you put your training at a lower level on certain days? Can you exercise at other times of the day than usual? Need to rest?
- Self-compassion. Pushing away emotions usually makes them come back in an even stronger form. An effective way to make the emotions subside faster is to meet yourself with self-compassion. What would you say to a friend who was feeling bad and spoke badly about himself? What would you have done? By treating ourselves with care instead of mean self-talk, strong emotions usually subside faster. It is enough with a "You do the best you can, and that is good enough."
- Activities that create well-being. When you feel mentally unwell, it can be difficult to find motivation and desire for activities that usually make you feel good. One method is to write down things that you like on a list. Nothing is too insignificant, and please be as specific as possible. For example, it can be to wear comfortable clothes, a certain scent, be in the woods, do puzzles, listen to a specific podcast, etc. When you're at your worst, take out your list and pick out at least one (preferably more!) thing you like, even if you may not have much desire at the moment. It is important that they match your energy level, are undemanding and not about performing. Often, both motivation and desire come back once you have started the activity.
