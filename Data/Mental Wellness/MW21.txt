## Eating Disorder: Common to many who have developed an eating disorder is an excessive preoccupation with thoughts about food, what to eat, body weight and body figure, as well as a strong fear of gaining weight. Being dissatisfied with one's body and strongly judgmental of how it looks, is the most central driving force in an eating disorder. Body weight and body shape become crucial for how the person values themselves. The eating disorders are not always visible on the outside, some are very thin, while others may have a normal body weight. Food is an important part of a sports venture and athletes are usually well aware of it. Athletes are also well aware of what their body looks like and how it works. This is not problematic in itself, as long as the athlete has a healthy approach to both food and their body, but for some athletes, the pursuit of the perfect body in that specific sport can become problematic. Research shows that eating disorders are more common among elite athletes than among non-athletes. In some sports, where low body weight (e.g. endurance sports), a specific body weight (e.g. sports with weight classes) or a slim figure (e.g. aesthetic sports) are central, there is a particularly high prevalence of eating disorders. If an athlete loses weight and is rewarded with, for example, faster times or higher scores, this reward can encourage them to lose even more weight, and an unhealthy process begins. Here it is important to realize that losing weight can lead to improved performance, but only in the short term. Too little food for a long time will always have a negative impact on performance.

## What can you do if you have an eating disorder?

Since eating disorders often creep up over time, it can be difficult to see and understand when it starts to be a disease. Therefore, we encourage you to seek medical care as soon as you recognize yourself in the symptoms above or suspect that you are starting to develop an eating disorder.

## You can try the tips below when your symptoms are very mild.
Tell them how you feel and about your thoughts about food and your body. It can help to articulate and express your feelings for someone else, such as a close relative or friend. Eat regular meals. If you try to avoid calories by skipping meals, you'll just get extra hungry within a few hours, which can lead to you overeating then and feeling sad about it again. Regular food intake (3 main meals and 2–3 snacks) is important to stabilize your blood sugar levels and thus make wise decisions more easily.
Pay attention to whether you usually compare your body and how much you eat with others. Such comparisons are common among people with eating disorders. They are part of the problem and do not make the problem any less. Be mindful and try to refrain from this behavior.
Pay attention to whether you have a tendency to look in the mirror, measure and assess your body. These behaviors are problematic because they confirm a possible dissatisfaction with the body.
