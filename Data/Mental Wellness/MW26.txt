## Worry in sport : Many parts of sport are uncertain. For example, we never know in advance how it will go, who will win or who will perform. Anxiety is something athletes are most likely to experience during their careers and need to learn to put up with. The fact that we have also lived in a time characterized by uncertainty and uncertainty since 2020 with the corona pandemic and the war in Ukraine, makes us all of us worrying. Worry can be described as a milder form of anxiety, where the physical symptoms are experienced more grinding. However, it is important to understand that it is normal and highly human to experience anxiety from time to time; It belongs to life and sport. There are various reasons why we feel worried. For example, it is common to worry about your health, that something will happen to your loved one or that you will have problems with your finances. The worry can also be about not knowing how your sports career is going, if you will succeed with the goals you have set. Of course, anxiety can also be about crises or threats that occur in the outside world and that frighten us.

Common symptoms that you can get when you are worried are that you have difficulty sleeping and can experience nightmares, you get stomach aches and tension in muscles, or you can experience that you become easily irritated or impatient. For some, worry can develop into anxiety.

It's important to understand that worry can be good for us. Then the worry can help us prepare for a task or performance, by adapting our behavior. An example is when you feel anxious about everything you need to take with you on a trip to a championship. Then the worry leads to some type of action, such as looking for a passport, checking that the passport is valid or collecting other important documents, which leads to the worry disappearing because the problem is solved. However, worry can also be negative. Then the worry contains things that cannot be solved and/or not to get full security in right now, but which you spend a lot of time and energy on by pondering. One example is that you get very worried about your performance at upcoming championships. It's not something that can be solved here and now – because the thing you're worried about is in the future. Then it can easily happen that your thoughts loop in the same paths and that you do not come up with anything new, no matter how much you ponder. It is the latter form of worry that can cause problems for us and can affect our well-being negatively. 

## What can you in case of worry? 

- Learn to distinguish between productive/positive worry and unreasonable, exaggerated and negative worry. To distinguish what is what, you can write down everything you worry about on a piece of paper, and divide it into two groups: 1) what you can influence and do something about right now, and 2) what you cannot influence and do something about right now. The latter is often about big issues (world events) and/or things that happen in the future (championships).

- For helpful concerns:
Solve the problem/situation that causes concern. If you find it difficult to find the solution yourself, a tip is to talk to someone you trust and who can support you in solving the problem.

For negative worry: Stop checking the worry when it appears: Worries that are not helpful to you are not based on problems that can be solved here and now, so the best thing is to let go of control. Challenge yourself to put up with the worry for a while, and feel free to adopt the attitude "it will go as it goes".Practice mindfulness: Practice noting your worrying thoughts, describe them and let them be as they are. Worrying thoughts can often sound like "what if...". The thought will, with time and practice, become less scary as long as it is allowed to be there. When a worrying thought comes up and you describe it, you can train yourself to focus on the present moment, on something that is going on around you (e.g., the conversation that is going on, the TV show that you are watching, sounds around you, etc.).
Accept your concern: Take note of when the worry appears, observe what is happening to you and allow yourself to feel that way. You can use your "worst thought", to dare to imagine the worst possible scenario you can think of, and do it without trying to calm yourself down (a common control strategy is to try to calm down or comfort yourself in different ways).When you worry about things that are happening in the world: Find out just the right amount of information - sometimes you may be able to completely refrain from the media and press, if it triggers your concern. You can try what happens to you if you take a day or two "break" from the media, note what happens to the worry and your well-being afterwards.Break the worry with an activity: If you feel a lot of anxiety, the feeling can prevent you from doing things that make you feel good. Therefore, try to plan activities and do things that you know you usually feel good about (e.g. cooking, baking, painting, reading a book, playing music, practicing mindfulness or calling a friend). Even if it doesn't feel good when you start, your worries are likely to change when you do something that you enjoy and your worries will decrease because they are given less space. Keep routines: It is easy for routines related to sleep, food and physical activity to change when we experience anxiety, and then it is common to end up in a vicious circle, as we are more likely to feel anxious if we are tired and hungry. When you feel that the worry has led to increased muscle tension: Learn and regularly perform some relaxation exercise (see some suggestions below under "exercises to calm your body").
