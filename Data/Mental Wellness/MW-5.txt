# There are five challenges that elite athletes can encounter during their careers: career transitions, dual careers, perfectionism, injuries, and overtraining. 

## CAREER TRANSITION:

The research shows that the end of the career of elite athletes can be a situation with an increased risk of experiencing psychological symptoms, especially when the career ends suddenly. Therefore, please have another occupation (e.g. a job, an education, a project) alongside your sports investment, so that you can more easily find another track when the career ends. If this is not possible, due to your investment, it may be good to think about what you would like to do after your career, for example by exploring different professions or reading about educations that you find interesting.

How can you deal with career transition?

If you feel that your mood is deteriorating due to changed circumstances in your sports venture, we recommend that you consider the following:
- Take care of yourself. Make sure you have regular eating and sleeping times and that you eat healthy foods. Minimize or avoid intake of alcohol or other substances.
- Book in activities that you enjoy, even if you may not feel like it at the moment. If you activate yourself with meaningful things, it can give you positive feelings. It can be about painting, cooking or going for walks in nature; simply something that you find fun.
- Invest in your social life. Contact your friends, try to meet new people or be close to your family. Social contacts and social support are an important factor in feeling well.
- Learn to manage thoughts and feelings in a good way, as this can be especially important in challenging phases where negative thoughts and feelings can dominate. In those situations, mindfulness is good.
- Find out your values: What is important to you, deep down?
- Talk to someone about how you feel, such as a family member, friend, or coach. Has anyone in your environment gone through a similar situation? Maybe you can exchange experiences with him?

## DUAL CAREER:
Dual careers are about not seeing a person as just an athlete, but having a holistic view of the person. It is recommended that elite athletes develop both as athletes and in other, non-athletic, areas in parallel throughout their sporting career. 
A dual career simply means that you will successfully start, develop, and complete your elite sports career as part of your life career. Having another occupation, for example studies or work, alongside your sports investment, is something we recommend.
There are several advantages to this, for example that you can develop several parts of your identity, that you can get to know other environments and acquire social relationships outside of your sport, and that you get the opportunity to develop more skills that you can benefit from through life. Having another job next to your sports venture also gives you the opportunity to broaden your perspectives and not only spend time and commitment on your sport.
## PERFECTIONISM:
A constant pursuit of improved performance and perfection is a central part of elite sport. A possible consequence of staying in this environment is therefore to develop perfectionist traits.Researchers have for a long time investigated perfectionism specifically in the context of performance and claim that there are two different parts of perfectionism. One part is precisely about what is described above: a quest to always be better and to set high goals. This part is considered good for elite athletes, as it makes it possible to constantly develop and improve. The second part contains what is called perfectionistic concerns – thoughts and feelings that revolve around the performance situation – for example thoughts that one is performing at a lower level than planned, anxiety about the next competition when there is a risk of failure, or a fear of being negatively evaluated unless everything is 100 percent perfect.
Athletes who have such perfectionist concerns to a large extent risk becoming stressed in the long run and experiencing strong anxiety before performance situations. That type of perfectionism is called maladaptive perfectionism . In general, it can be said that there is a greater risk for elite athletes with maladaptive perfectionism to experience symptoms of mental illness.
If you are experiencing symptoms of mental illness, you may want to ask yourself if a perfectionist mindset may have caused you to develop these symptoms. An example of a symptom is having difficulty relaxing in the evening and falling asleep. If it is due to perfectionistic traits that lead to high stress in your life, you are advised to address your perfectionism first. Your symptoms will probably improve or disappear completely with it.
## How can you deal with perfectionism?
If you feel that your perfectionist traits are making you feel bad, we recommend that you consider the following:
- Think about areas in life where it might be good for you to have high standards and in which areas you might be able to lower your standards. Is it reasonable that everything you do should be perfect? How will you feel in the long run if you strive for perfection in everything you do? Choose one or more areas in life where you try to lower your demands and have the attitude that "good enough" is enough.
- Think about which goals and requirements are reasonable to reach and which are unreasonable to reach, when it comes to the areas that are important in your life, and where you want to have high goals. If you set too high goals time and time again, you will be disappointed. Therefore, try to adjust the goals so that they become more realistic, so that you have a chance to achieve the goals and feel satisfied. You may be able to check your goals with someone else, for example your coach, to get a "reality check" on whether the goal is realistic or not.
- Challenge your ideas by testing them in real life and see if they are right. If you're worried about negative consequences if something goes wrong, it may be because you blackmail the consequences, without questioning their validity. For example, you can try making mistakes on purpose, or doing something less than perfect, and see what happens. Will the consequences really be as dire as you thought? For example, if you always read an email ten times before sending it, to make sure every single letter is correct, try sending it straight away next time. Feel free to write down what you think will happen before you send the email, and evaluate afterwards what actually happened. Were the consequences different than you thought? What are the benefits of doing things without overworking them?
- Learn to become aware of your perfectionist concerns, to be able to deal with them in the best way. How do your thoughts sound before a task and how do they sound after you complete a task? What emotions are you experiencing? Mindfulness training can be good for increasing awareness.
- Practice self-compassion, an approach that involves treating yourself kindly in daily life, and especially in periods of adversity. Self-compassion is a good approach to practice if you have perfectionist traits.

## INJURIES:
Most athletes suffer a sports injury at some point during their career. An injury can lead to everything from you needing to adapt your training for a certain period of time or being away from training and competition for several weeks or months, to being forced to interrupt your career due to the injury.
It is common to feel a little down when you are injured and to feel fear of suffering new injuries when it is time to return to training and competition. If these feelings are experienced as mild and transient and limited to the injury itself, there is nothing to worry about - they are completely normal reactions and something to be accepted. However, there is research that shows that injured elite athletes have an increased risk of developing more serious mental conditions, such as depression and anxiety. To prevent this, it can be good to take care of your well-being at an early stage after an injury (see tips below).

An injury that occurred in a traumatic way (e.g. through an accident) can mean that the athlete develops strong psychological reactions and memories. 
A concussion is a serious injury that, in the worst case, can lead to neurological symptoms (eg memory disorders) or psychological symptoms (eg depression) throughout life. To reduce the risk of serious and long-term symptoms, it is important to be careful with the rehabilitation. Talk to your doctor about how to do it.
### How can you deal with injuries?

Here are some tips on how you can work with your mental health during a period of injury:
- Book activities into your everyday life. For example, it could be meeting a friend, visiting a friend, painting, photographing - simply activities that you find fun and meaningful. Even if you feel sad or down and don't feel like doing anything, it's important not to let your emotions rule and not to get caught up in brooding about your situation. Instead, do things that you usually enjoy, so that you have the opportunity to experience positive emotions.
- Accept the situation as it is, it is an important step. This does not mean that you have to like the situation, but you have to accept that the injury occurred, in order to move forward with your rehabilitation.
- Develop your ability to deal with negative thoughts and feelings. It can be especially important in periods of injury when negative thoughts and feelings often dominate. Mindfulness training can be good for this purpose.
- Explore your values: What is important to you, deep down?
- Use the time you have, when you cannot train and compete, for activities that you would otherwise never have time for. Maybe you want to study a course at college or university? Or visit a family member who lives further away?
-If you feel like it, keep in touch with your training buddies by visiting them at training or by meeting them in other ways.
-Ask if there is a task you can take over at the club, for example as an assistant coach or analyst.
-It is common for the day structure to change due to an injury. Make sure to get a clear daily structure, for example by having fixed times for eating and sleeping. Also make sure you don't stay in bed too long in the morning, but schedule some activity so you get up and start the day.

## OVERTRAINING:
A major challenge that elite athletes face is finding the balance between, on the one hand, training load and the psychosocial stresses that exist in the athlete's life, and on the other hand, sufficient recovery. A training load that is too low does not develop performance, while a training load that is too heavy results in impaired performance, due to a so-called overtraining problem.
When an athlete overexerts themselves over a long period of time and recovers too little (a condition known as overtraining syndrome), this can lead not only to impaired performance, but also to psychological symptoms. Overtraining syndrome can present with symptoms common to depression, such as low mood, fatigue, sleep problems, changes in appetite and weight, problems with motivation and difficulty concentrating.
If overtraining syndrome is detected early, there are good opportunities to regain full performance with the right strategies. However, repeated phases of overtraining syndrome with inadequate recovery can lead to burnout. Therefore, it is important that you learn what your load-recovery balance looks like and be alert when it is disturbed, so that you can act at an early stage.

### How can you deal with overtraining ?
If you feel that the balance between recovery and load is disturbed, you can use the following tips:
- Discuss your situation with your coach or someone on the medical team. It is usually an important first step to take.
- Make sure you sleep long enough and that your sleep is perceived as satisfactory.
- Book in recovery activities. Elite athletes are often quite good at planning training, competitions, trips and work or school assignments. However, recovery is not planned to the same extent. Plan a day or a whole week in advance and make sure you get some free periods that you can use for recovery. Enter these activities in your calendar, just as you enter anything else.
- Think about what recovery means to you. There is no recipe for what optimal recovery looks like, but you have to base it entirely on your own experience. Some athletes enjoy going for walks, spending time in nature, having coffee with friends, going to the cinema, reading a book or taking a sauna. List some restorative activities that you can choose from and then schedule them into your everyday life.
- No restorative activity becomes relaxing when your mind wanders off to something else. Therefore, mindfulness training - where you learn to be here and now - can be a good complement. Regular mindfulness practice not only teaches you to be in the here and now as you recover, it has also been shown to reduce stress.
- Learn different relaxation exercises that you can use when you need to replenish your energy.
- Take a training break, anywhere from a day to 1-2 weeks (depending on how tired you feel). Better to rest a few extra days than too little, because then it will probably just take longer to get back into balance.
